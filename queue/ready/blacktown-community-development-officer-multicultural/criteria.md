## Please describe your experience and knowledge working with culturally and linguistically diverse communities.

For the past four years my work has been community engagement with culturally
and linguistically diverse communities across Greater Western Sydney. At ACON I
design and deliver peer-led health promotion for multicultural communities,
reaching 300+ participants a year.

Two pieces of work show how I go about it. I led the consultation, development
and delivery of Start Making Sense, a workshop supporting LGBTQ+ Middle Eastern
men to navigate culture and sexuality. I identified the community need, ran a
series of consultations to shape the content, and worked with Middle Eastern
community members to write, advertise and run it. Part of doing that
responsibly was recognising the risk in bringing together participants of
different faiths during a period of high community tension, escalating it
rather than hoping it would hold, and supporting volunteers who were affected.
The workshop ran, and the connection it built is sustained through a
volunteer-led social group.

I also co-led Starting Out West, a workshop for multicultural and neurodiverse
young adults in Western Sydney. It reached a priority population that had not
previously accessed ACON's services. That happened because we identified the
real barriers, geographic distance and cultural safety, and worked with a
Western Sydney partner organisation to host the workshop in a space those
communities already trusted. It has since expanded in content and location.

Knowledge matters as much as delivery here. I reviewed our participant
demographic data, found multicultural community members were under-represented,
and used research together with community consultation to design a different
initiative that reached them through visual arts and social connection rather
than a health framing that was not landing. I facilitated a consultation with
multicultural young people to inform the Australian Human Rights Commission's
National Anti-Racism Framework, and I advise research teams at UNSW, Western
Sydney University and Monash University on how to engage these communities
well.

I also bring lived experience of this, having grown up queer and Vietnamese in
South-West Sydney. It informs the work; it does not substitute for it.

## Please outline your experience working as a member of a team to deliver collaborative outcomes.

Most of what I deliver is delivered with other people, and the outcomes I am
proudest of are the collaborative ones.

The clearest example is a resource setting out ACON's priority populations peer
work initiatives. I coordinated across 9 teams to build it, gathering what each
team needed represented and reconciling that into one document that reflected
each community's own language. Nine teams is nine sets of priorities and nine
review cycles, and the work was as much negotiation and sequencing as it was
writing.

Starting Out West is the other. I co-led it with ACON's Multicultural and
Greater Western Sydney Community Engagement Team, which meant working across
two teams with different remits and then out to an external Western Sydney
organisation for the venue. The collaborative outcome outlasted the workshop:
the partnership we built has since extended into other ACON projects, which to
me is the real test of whether a collaboration was worth anyone's time.

I also work alongside ACON's priority population teams, including the First
Nations Health Program, the Women's Health Program and the Asian Gay, Bi+,
Queer Men's Project, contributing to project plans, facilitating sessions and
supporting outreach. I am an active member of ACON's Reconciliation Action Plan
Champions, a cross-organisational group progressing Reconciliation Action Plan
deliverables, which is committee work in the same shape as supporting an
advisory committee.

Team delivery also means the unglamorous half. I recruit, train and coordinate a
volunteer workforce of 60+ peer educators, book the venues and equipment,
process budget paperwork, keep the participant data current, and take the
agendas and minutes. Programs run on that, and someone has to do it properly.

Earlier, at Western Sydney University, I worked as part of a research team and
was second author on three peer-reviewed papers, which is collaborative writing
under other people's deadlines as much as my own.

## Please describe your experience using Microsoft Office and other computer applications, including how you quickly learn and adapt to new systems and software.

I use Microsoft Office daily and across the full range of what the work
demands. Word for evaluation reports, facilitator manuals and academic papers.
Excel for participant data, program records and the evaluation analysis my team
uses to decide what to change. PowerPoint for presentations to internal teams
and external partners. I work equally in the Google Workspace equivalents,
since partner organisations often live there.

Beyond Office, the applications I use regularly are Google Forms and
SurveyMonkey for evaluation and consultation instruments, Eventbrite for
registration and participant management, Zoom for online delivery, and Adobe
Illustrator and Photoshop for the programs' visual material.

The system that matters most to this question is Penelope, the client
information system I conduct participant intakes in. That work involves
sensitive personal information, so learning it properly was not optional. I use
it to record intakes, identify participants who may need a different setting
than a peer group, and refer people to other services.

On learning new systems: my honest answer is that I learn them by needing them.
Penelope, Eventbrite and SurveyMonkey were all systems I picked up because a
piece of work required them, not from a training course. My research assistant
role is where this habit formed. I was analysing qualitative and quantitative
data across concurrent projects, which meant moving between survey data,
interview transcripts and workshop material and using whatever tool each
required.

I would rather say plainly that I have not worked in a local government records
or corporate system, so Council's own platforms would be new to me. What I can
say is that I have learned every system this work has put in front of me,
including one holding confidential client information, and I am comfortable
troubleshooting rather than waiting for someone to come and fix it.

## Please explain how you establish and maintain close working relationships with key stakeholders.

I establish relationships by being useful to the other organisation's purposes
as well as my own, and I maintain them by following through on small things
reliably enough that the large things become possible.

The partnerships I hold at ACON span Local Health Districts, multicultural and
faith-based organisations, mental health services, universities and migrant
resource services, including Head to Health and the Western Sydney Migrant
Resource Centre. Some began because we needed something. Most have lasted
because we were also worth having as a partner.

Starting Out West is the example I would point a panel to. We needed a venue
that multicultural young adults would actually walk into, so I approached a
Western Sydney organisation those communities already trusted. Getting the
venue was the easy part. What made it a relationship rather than a transaction
was that the work we did together was good, and the partnership has since
extended into other ACON projects that have nothing to do with me. That is the
outcome I aim for.

Maintaining relationships with community members works the same way and is
harder. Running Start Making Sense meant holding the trust of participants and
volunteers of different faiths through a period when tensions outside the room
were high. I did that by being straight with people about what the workshop
was, taking concerns seriously the first time they were raised, and escalating
risks rather than managing them quietly.

I also represent the organisation upward. I facilitated a consultation with
multicultural young people that fed the Australian Human Rights Commission's
National Anti-Racism Framework, and I advise research teams at UNSW, Western
Sydney University and Monash University on engaging these communities.
Stakeholder work in a role like this runs in both directions, toward the
community and toward government and sector partners, and I have done both.

What I have not done is hold stakeholder relationships inside local government,
where the accountabilities and the committee structures differ from the
not-for-profit sector. I would need to learn Council's, and I would rather say
so than imply otherwise.
